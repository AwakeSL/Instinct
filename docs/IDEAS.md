# Every idea, and what it measured

**Shared document.** More than one model writes here. Conventions: read the whole file before proposing; add a
proposal as a row under *Proposed, untested* with your name and date; move it to *Kept* or *Tried and dropped*
only with a number from a run and a section in `FINDINGS.md`; never rewrite or delete another writer's row;
append a note under it if you disagree. The log is the evidence; this file is the index.

One line per idea: what it was, the number it got, the verdict, and the section of `docs/FINDINGS.md` (the full
running log) with the detail. Read this before proposing anything; most obvious ideas are here with a number.

**How to read the numbers.** *Arena* = the abilities arena (`tabffa3.lua`), sum of four learners' check damage
per seed, curriculum baseline 1,852 over three seeds (or 588 a seed over eight). *Held-out* = the generated worlds
never tuned on (`tabgen.lua`, worlds 18 / 20 / 24 / 28), mean of the last ten episodes over six seeds, baseline
187 / 704 / 314 / 207. *Duel* = one creature against a scripted fighter, wins of 30. Lower is worse everywhere.

## Open, with the evidence so far

- Learning cost is the square of the sense count and every linear-cost variant costs about a quarter of the
  learning (76-78). The sense budget is about sixty a creature at full quality; the decision rate is the one free
  lever (79).
- Newborn reliability: one seed in three still learns a world late even with the coach (67-68).
- Knowledge transfer works through live weights and tag-level priors, not through distilled weight files on new
  worlds (63, 73).
- Where the tick actually goes, profiled on world 2 at 37 senses and 80 combinations (`scripts/prof_mind.luau`,
  Lune; Studio native about 3x): think 155 us, settle 506 us, 661 us a tick. Of the learning, the thirteen effects
  models (F=112, NR=37, four updated a tick) are 73%, the outcome model 22%, the couplings 5%. The effects
  covariance is the cost, and 49, 59, 62 and 78 all say it is the part that will not be shared or thinned.
- Every attempt to make the existing computation cheaper by *approximating* it has lost, without exception:
  diagonal, shared and pooled effects covariance (49, 59, 62), block-local and sparse by slot or value (76, 77),
  ridge 12, subsampled rows, narrowed effects inputs, gated outputs, effects for move+act only (77, 78). About
  fifteen variants, no survivors. The covariance is doing real work and the cost is irreducible at the model's
  current *width*. What has never been tried is making the model narrower.
- The mind is append-only: `RLS.grow` is the only structural operation and nothing is ever evicted, so a creature
  can only ever get slower. The sixty-sense budget is a consequence of that, not of RLS.
- **The held-out benchmark cannot resolve anything smaller than about a doubling, and most of the tables above are
  inside its noise.** Measured on `tabgen.lua` at 150 episodes, last-ten-episode score, three seeds a world:
  world 18 gives 184 / 47 / 86, world 20 gives **59 / 944 / 207**, world 24 gives 388 / 576 / 28, world 28 gives
  390 / 59 / 419. Per-world coefficients of variation are 67 / 118 / 84 / 69%, a mean of **84%**. The smallest
  difference an unpaired comparison can detect at that spread is about 138% of baseline at three seeds an arm,
  **97% at six**, 69% at twelve and 44% at thirty. The 6-seed means the tables quote therefore separate only
  effects that roughly double or halve the score; differences of 10-50%, which is most of what is recorded as a
  win or a loss, are not distinguishable from a reseed. This is the most likely explanation for ideas in the log
  reversing between waves, and for the same idea reading as a win on one world and a loss on its neighbour.
  Three cheap fixes, in order of value: (1) **analyse paired** -- a seed fixes the world draw, so the same seeds
  in both arms turn a between-groups comparison into a within-pair one and cost nothing extra; (2) quote the
  spread or the per-seed numbers, never a bare mean; (3) check whether rows-to-threshold, which `tabgen` also
  reports, has a lower CV than last-ten score -- if it does, it is the better yardstick and every future
  comparison should use it. Until then, treat any single-digit or low-double-digit percentage in these tables as
  unmeasured rather than measured. (Lume, 2026-09-04, from the twelve-pair battery behind the reliability-weighted
  value row.)
- **`scripts/mind.luau` has only one live reading**, and it is a trap for anything touching the value. `totalsOf`
  returns `{ 0, 0, w.eaten }`, so `dealt` and `taken` are identically zero in both worlds and their variance, and
  hence `RLS.confidence`, is exactly 0. Nothing about the preference sum can be discriminated there. Worse, the
  value's *scale* is not free: `surprise`, `memGood` and `reps` compare a prediction against the raw score, so any
  change that rescales the value silently becomes a replay-rate change. Measured the hard way -- a value inflated
  3x by a careless normalisation read as +17% on world 24 and -11% on world 2, and the cause was `reps = 3` firing
  on every good row, not the intended change. Use `tabgen.lua` (five readings, four of them live) for anything
  about the objective; a `tabmind3` copy reproduces the ledger baseline exactly (world 18 seed 1: 184 vs 187).
- *Why* the covariance will not be shared or transported (61, 62, 78), measured rather than inferred: a covariance
  is a **policy-conditional** object. Each option's rows come from its own region of the input space, because the
  policy picks the option *from* the state, so a pooled covariance is not a rescaling of any one option's -- it is
  the wrong metric for all of them. In a synthetic effects model at F=106 with the option drawn *uniformly*,
  sharing one covariance across five option models is free (R2 0.9953 vs 0.9956 own); with the option chosen *from
  the state*, the same sharing triples the residual error (0.9470 vs 0.9824). That one mechanism predicts four
  separate losses -- shared effects covariance (78), pooling across creatures (62), spawning from the source's
  covariance (61) and rebirth keeping it (62): different options, different creatures, different generations and a
  reborn policy all visit different states. It also says what is safe: *thinning* a covariance in place is fair
  game, *moving* one across a distribution shift is not. This is the mechanism behind the slot-equivariant note's
  instinct to tie weights while leaving each model its own covariance. (Bingo, 2026-09-04)
- Without a flag-by-sense term, the outcome model's greedy policy is **state-blind**, and this follows from
  `phiO` alone rather than from any measurement. The inputs are `[1, X, flags, descriptors, acquired]`. Comparing
  two options of the same choice at the same state, every term cancels except that choice's flags, so the
  difference is `w_flag[o1] - w_flag[o2]` -- a **constant**, with no `X` in it. For the `move` choice, which does
  not change `X`, the outcome model therefore prefers the same move no matter what the creature senses. Every bit
  of state-dependence a creature has comes from somewhere else: the target choice (which does change `X`, via
  `sensesOf`), the step-ahead term, acquired features that happen to pair a flag with a sense, and the descriptor
  cross terms. That reframes several rows above as one thing rather than four. Per-context option weights (32, 56)
  are a hand-cut flag-by-context interaction, and they were needed. Revisable descriptors (72, 73) beating private
  flags at 8, 32 and 100 acts is what a rank-D factorisation of the flag-by-sense interaction looks like when the
  alternative is having none. 67's flag variants all losing is what happens when you weaken the only decisive
  block while leaving it just as state-blind. And a full interaction is `nOpt * nX` inputs, which is why the
  factorised form is the only affordable one. The prediction: descriptors are not an acts-side nicety, they are
  the *only* cheap route to a state-dependent choice, and they should be on for every choice with more than a
  couple of options -- including `move` in the food trials, where they are currently off. Cheap to test, and it
  says where the model's width is actually worth spending. (Bingo, 2026-09-04)
- Putting four of the rows below together says where the value is, and it is not the clock. The outcome model's
  greedy policy is **state-blind** without a flag-by-sense term (the note above). In the food trials descriptors
  are off and acquisition finds at most a handful of pairs, so for the `move` choice essentially the *only* route
  to a state-dependent decision is the step-ahead term. And that term is miscalibrated three independent ways:
  its confidence is applied twice, the second time to an absolute value whose sign decides whether an untrained
  option is punished or rewarded; the per-option effects each carry the baseline drift, so summing them over
  nChoices multiplies the baseline (measured slope **0.548** where 1.000 is honest); and on the legacy path the
  confidence average is diluted by absent slots. So the one thing making the creature's move depend on what it
  senses is being inflated, arbitrarily biased, and damped, all at once. That reframes 37's two-step lookahead and
  70's longer chains: chaining a step with three compounding calibration errors is expected to lose, and those
  results are evidence about the calibration rather than about lookahead depth. The prediction was that fixing the three
  would be worth more than any speed change on this list. **It was tested and it is false on world 2** -- all
  three, alone and together, land inside the noise (see the MEASURED row below). The defects are real; their
  consequences on this world are not. Left standing: the *structural* half of this note -- that the greedy
  policy is state-blind without a flag-by-sense term -- which is a separate claim and still untested.
  (Bingo, 2026-09-04)
- The "no survivors" bullet above is about *approximations*. The untried class is **identities** -- changes that
  return the same numbers to the last bit and only move the clock. Learning cannot move, so they need a stopwatch,
  not six seeds. Separable pick search is one; four more are proposed below (matvec zero-skipping, repeated rows in
  closed form, the memory prefilter, and the drift block in the outcome model). (Bingo, 2026-09-04)

### Proposed, not yet measured

Four ideas that survive a read of the tables above. All must beat the held-out baseline 187/704/314/207.

- **Slot-equivariant effects.** One law a slot-*kind*, weights tied across the slot groups `Senses.groups` already
  names, each slot read in its own relative frame. Effects outputs fall from every sense to one slot's worth plus
  the globals, and every slot's rows train the same parameters: about 4x fewer effects parameters and 4x the rows a
  parameter, so it should *raise* learning if the law really is one law, and flatten the slots and lose if they
  differ in kind. Untried: 56-57 kept slot *senses*, not tied weights, and 76-77 partitioned the covariance, never
  the weights. Note this ties weights and leaves each model its own covariance, which is what 78 says not to touch.
  It also opens the cheap route to bonds: a few learned numbers a remembered individual, carried in the slot as
  extra senses and read by the shared law, so the physics stays general and the relationship stays specific.

- **Separable pick search.** `phiO` makes the value additive in the picks: option flags are one-hot per choice and
  the descriptor terms sum over choices (`acc[q]` is a sum), so value = base(X) + sum over choices of w[option],
  exactly. Only acquired features pairing two flags from *different* choices break it, and those are enumerable. So
  the best combination is the per-choice argmax, once per target option, plus a correction over the cross terms and
  a feasibility repair; k-best per-choice lists feed the beam unchanged. This is an identity, not a prune -- 37
  killed approximate pruning and hierarchical think, but this returns the same combination, so learning cannot
  move and only the clock should change. The first pass then costs the target options rather than their product,
  so a thousand combinations cost what twenty do. Measure against the attention gate (48), which already skips
  60-70% of thinks and so caps what this can be worth.

- **Deferred learning, not lazier learning.** Rows already settle a horizon late; nothing requires the four effects
  updates, the outcome update and the couplings update to land on one tick. Push them as jobs and drain to a
  microsecond budget a tick: cost becomes the budget, flat and independent of the sense count, and a spike becomes
  a backlog instead of a dropped frame. Distinct from every neighbouring loss -- error-gated learning, lazy effects
  outputs, couplings every 4th tick (71, 77) and shared physics from 1 row in 2 or 4 (78) all *discard* rows, and a
  draining queue discards none. The risk is exactly that: under sustained load a queue that never drains decays
  into the subsampling 78 measured at about a quarter of the learning, so the test has to report the backlog
  alongside the score.

- **A learned sense code (senses as descriptors).** 72 proved a compact learned code beats an explicit declared
  vocabulary on the act side -- 68/5/306 against -71/-101/182 for flags at 8/32/100 acts, the margin widening with
  the vocabulary, and the sparse fifty-tag version losing to the compact graded one, so the win was compression
  itself. Senses today are what act flags were: one declared slot a thing, all dense, all read by every model, the
  width set by the designer. Have the models read a learned code of B ~ 12-24 rather than the 37 senses (111
  extended), fitted on the value residual by the gradient `Mind:descrStep` already computes -- shipped machinery
  aimed at a second target, not new machinery. Cost becomes B squared, fixed and independent of how many senses the
  game offers, so senses stop being a budget and become candidates: three hundred would cost what 37 costs now. On
  the profile above, B=16 takes an effects update from about 33,400 units to about 6,400 and settle from 506 to
  ~120 us, B=12 to ~70 us. It also gives eviction a home, which the append-only mind has never had: a fixed-size
  basis makes features compete instead of accumulate. Composes with the slot-equivariant proposal above -- the same
  encoder applied per slot with tied weights *is* one law a slot-kind, and a bond becomes a few learned numbers
  carried in the slot beside the code. Risks: it puts a nonlinearity in front of a linear model, and 37, 54 and 58
  are unkind to added machinery; the defence is that it adds coordinates, not search, and the one previous change
  of that kind (72) won. Watch for the code and the weights chasing each other -- 37 says forgetting broke the
  covariance -- so move the code slowly against the weights and reset only the affected rows of P on a swap.
  Cheapest first test: a *fixed random* projection to B dims. If a random code holds most of the baseline the
  bottleneck is viable and learning it can only improve on that; if random collapses, the idea dies for one run.
- **A Kronecker-factored covariance (`P ~ A (x) B` on the slot-by-field grid).** About fifteen ways of making P
  cheaper have lost (49, 59, 62, 76, 77, 78) and they share one property: diagonal, block-local, sparse-by-slot and
  sparse-by-value all *delete* entries of P, and 76's block-local numbers (37/628/164/77) say the deleted entries
  are where the learning lives. A Kronecker factorization deletes nothing. After 80 the effects inputs are an
  explicit grid -- S slots by F fields -- so write `P = A (x) B`, with A the S-by-S covariance across slots and B
  the F-by-F covariance across fields: every one of the (S*F)^2 entries still exists and is still nonzero, but it is
  constrained to the product `A[s][s'] * B[f][f']`. Cost an update falls from (S*F)^2 to S^2 + F^2 -- at 318 senses
  the 2,370 us settle that 80 measured should fall by roughly an order. It is also not 78's shared covariance
  across effects models (174/551/113/159), which forced *one* P on thirteen models; here every model keeps its own
  P and only its factorization is constrained. The update is the matrix-variate flip-flop step -- fix B, update A
  on the rows whitened by B, then swap -- and the known failure mode is that the factorization is exact only when
  the slot-to-slot and field-to-field correlations really are separable. 80 is evidence that they nearly are: tying
  one law across slots *raised* learning, which is the stronger form of the same assumption. Cheapest first test is
  static and costs no run: dump a converged full P from a logged episode, compute its nearest Kronecker product (an
  SVD of the reshaped P) and report the relative error. If a rank-1 Kronecker fit holds most of P's mass the online
  version is worth building; if it does not, the idea dies for the price of one offline SVD.

- **Kronecker-structured weights (NKP): the dial that 80 does not have.** 80 tied one law across the slot groups
  and won (155/815/216/152). A tie is the corner of a family: writing the weight grid as `w[s][f] = a[s]*b[f]` is
  the same law across every slot with a *learned* per-slot gain, and 80's hard tie is that with every `a[s]` frozen
  at 1. The adaptive-filtering literature calls this nearest-Kronecker-product identification and runs it as two
  short RLS filters updated alternately, at L1^2 + L2^2 instead of (L1*L2)^2. The rank-r form `w = sum_{i<=r} a_i
  (x) b_i` then interpolates continuously from the hard tie (r=1, a=1) to fully independent slots (r=min(S,F)), so
  for the first time the tie is a knob rather than a yes/no. That matters because 80 already showed the tie is not
  free: without the pooled slot context it read 122/750/126/49, so slots do differ, and the pool was the patch.
  `a[s]` is the principled form of that patch -- it lets a slot matter more without letting it have its own physics
  -- and r=2 lets two laws coexist. A recent study of low-rank full-matrix preconditioners on generalized linear
  models found the useful rank was consistently 1-2, which is the range to try first. Falsifiable prediction: r=1
  with a learned `a` is at least 80's numbers, and r=2 beats them. If r=1-learned *loses* to 80's frozen tie, then
  the pooled context is doing something a per-slot gain cannot, and that is worth knowing on its own.
- **Posterior-sampled exploration: noise on the targets, not a bonus on the value.** Six exploration rows have
  lost -- the spread/curiosity bonus (24, 37, 66), the improvement and revelation bonuses (24, 34), the untried-
  option bonus and lifelong randomness at 2% and 5% (67), sampled flags (67), twenty exploration episodes instead
  of five (66) -- and every one is of two kinds: a *deterministic bonus added to the value*, or *per-step dither*.
  The randomized-value-function literature says both kinds are the wrong shape, for reasons the tables already
  show. A bonus needs one global scale that is right in every world at once, which is exactly the failure 66
  recorded -- "fixes 28, collapses 24 (any strength)". Per-step dither is provably exponentially bad wherever a
  hypothesis has to be held long enough to be tested, because independent noise cancels itself out. RLSVI is
  neither: perturb the regression *targets* with Gaussian noise, draw the perturbation once an episode, then act
  greedily on the weights that result. The value keeps its form, no bonus term exists anywhere, and the greedy
  policy is untouched -- only the data the model fits is jittered, so what comes out is a draw from the model's own
  posterior instead of an optimistic tilt somebody had to scale by hand.

  Three things make it cheap here rather than speculative. It costs one Gaussian an update and no new state. It
  **does not touch `P`**: the covariance recursion depends on `phi` alone and never on the target, so the
  conditioning provably cannot degrade -- the property 37, 62 and 76-78 keep punishing other ideas for lacking,
  and the sharpest difference from the process-noise row, which perturbs `P` for plasticity where this perturbs
  the target for exploration. The two are orthogonal and could both run. And the noise scale is not a free
  parameter: the standard schedule shrinks as `sqrt(beta/(n+1))` in a feature's visit count, and the mind already
  tracks confidence per weight, so the schedule reads off state that exists.

  It also matches what the creature already does and is already rewarded for. Sticky exploration and the stuck
  nudge (74, both kept) hold a *random move*; the coach (68, kept) rebirths and re-explores, which is a crude
  resample of the whole value. Holding a random *hypothesis* for an episode is that same principle one level up,
  and it aims squarely at the open note that one seed in three still learns a world late even with the coach.
  Staged and falsifiable: `sigma = 0` must reproduce the baseline bit for bit, which is the whole harness, and
  then it is a one-scalar sweep. If a held, coherent draw loses the same way the bonuses did, the problem is not
  the *shape* of the exploration and that entire line closes for good -- worth a run for that alone.
- **The effective dimension is already sitting in the covariance, for free.** Two open rows need a number nobody
  has: the learned-sense-code row found the projection cliff between B=12 and B=16 by *sweeping* for it, and the
  eviction row ranks which input to drop but never says how many may go. Both are asking for the same quantity, and
  the model already stores it. `RLS.new` sets `P = I/ridge`, so after any number of rows `P = (G + ridge*I)^-1`
  exactly, with `G = sum phi phi'`. The statistical dimension of a ridge problem -- `d_eff = tr(G (G + lambda I)^-1)`,
  the standard effective degrees of freedom -- then collapses:

      d_eff = tr(G P) = tr((P^-1 - ridge*I) P) = F - ridge * tr(P)

  and `tr(P)` is a sum down a diagonal that is already in memory. **O(F) a read, no new state, exact.** It says how
  many of the F directions the data has actually paid for; `F - d_eff` is how many it has not.

  Three things fall out of having it. It *predicts* B rather than sweeping for it: the sketching result is that an
  embedding of size proportional to the statistical dimension preserves the regression, so `d_eff` says where the
  cliff is -- in every world separately, at any moment in a life -- instead of once by hand on worlds 2 and 24. It
  hands the evictor its budget: the covariance rule ranks *which* input is dead, `F - d_eff` says *how many* are,
  and the two together are a whole rule where each alone is half of one. And it is a saturation meter: `d_eff`
  still climbing means the creature is finding structure, `d_eff` flat while F grows means width is being paid for
  and not used, which is the first direct measurement of the append-only complaint in the Open notes rather than an
  argument about it.

  It also cross-checks against a row already in this table. `RLS.update` computes `denom = 1 + phi' P phi`, and the
  row leverage `phi' P phi / denom` -- exactly the `k` the novelty-gated downdate proposes to threshold -- **sums
  over the rows seen to `d_eff`**. That gate is therefore already computing this quantity's per-row increment, and
  accumulating it costs one add. Two independent routes to the same number, which must agree: a free correctness
  check on both.

  One caveat to state rather than bury: the identity is exact on the dense path. Under `absent` or `blocks` the
  update skips entries, so `P^-1` is no longer `G + ridge*I`, and `F - ridge*tr(P)` then measures the dimension of
  what the model actually *accumulated* rather than of the rows it was shown. For every use above that is the
  number you want anyway -- but it is not the textbook quantity and should not be reported as one.
- **The covariance as a flat `buffer` -- proposed, measured, and dead.** The argument was that `P` is
  `{ { number } }`, so `P[i]` is a pointer chase and each element is a 16-byte tagged value rather than 8 bytes of
  double; at F=318 that is ~1.6 MB against 809 KB for a flat f64 `buffer`, and if the update were memory-bound the
  clock would follow the bytes. I benchmarked the real inner loops -- `Pphi` then the rank-1 downdate -- as tables,
  as an f64 buffer and as an f32 buffer, in Lune and again in Studio.

      F=40    table   27.5 us | buf f64   32.1 us (0.86x) | buf f32   31.2 us (0.88x)
      F=112   table  179.1 us | buf f64  244.1 us (0.73x) | buf f32  254.2 us (0.70x)
      F=318   table 1559.4 us | buf f64 1315.7 us (1.19x) | buf f32 1527.4 us (1.02x)

  Buffers **lose** at every width the creature actually runs at, by 14-30%, and win only 1.19x at 318 -- and f32
  wins nothing anywhere (1.02x at its best). Lune agreed within noise at all four widths I tried there (40, 112,
  318, 640: 0.70x, 0.81x, 1.02x, 0.95x). The premise was simply wrong: cost tracks F^2 cleanly from 40 to 640
  (640^2/318^2 = 4.05 against a measured 3.9x) with no cache cliff anywhere, so the update is dispatch-bound and
  not bandwidth-bound, and Luau's array-part read is already a single fast opcode while `buffer.readf64` is not.

  That kills the follow-on too, and worth saying so explicitly: the f32 half was the interesting half, because f32
  is where the rank-1 downdate loses positive-definiteness and where Bierman's `P = U D U'` update earns its keep
  (single-precision UD matching a double-precision conventional filter at negligible extra CPU is a real and
  settled result). But UD is only worth importing if single precision buys speed, and here it buys none. Caveat on
  my own numbers: I could not confirm `--!native` was applied to the chunk `execute_luau` compiled, so the Studio
  figures may be interpreter figures as well -- but native would have to reverse a 27% deficit at F=112, not
  merely close it, and the shipped effects models run at F=112. Recorded as closed unless someone measures a
  genuinely native-compiled version and sees something different.

- **Parallel Luau: shard by kind, one Actor a kind.** The profile is per creature a tick; a server with N creatures
  pays N times it, and Roblox has had real multithreading for years that nothing here uses. The shape of the mind
  decides the sharding, and it decides it narrowly. Actors do not share a Luau VM: each `require` gives an actor
  its own copy of a module's state, so two actors cannot hold the same model. The one supported channel is
  `SharedTable`, and it is disqualified on numbers, not on taste -- a write costs about 3-10 us, and an update at
  F=318 does order F^2 = 100,000 element writes, so a single covariance update through a SharedTable would cost
  seconds. Record that as closed.

  What survives is coarse sharding along the seam the design already has. Shared effects models are per *kind*
  (50), so put each kind in its own Actor: every creature of that kind shares its models inside one VM exactly as
  it does today, the arithmetic is unchanged and the results are identical, and kinds run on separate threads for
  free. A game with four species gets close to four-way parallelism for no algorithmic risk at all. The honest
  limit is a game with one kind, where this buys nothing; there the only route is splitting a kind across shards,
  and that is not free -- 50 measured what sharing effects models across a kind is worth, so two shards halve that
  sharing, which makes it a measurable tradeoff rather than a win. Worth writing down because the alternative
  people reach for first -- an Actor a creature, models in a SharedTable -- is the one the numbers above rule out.
- **Choose which world to replay, not how many to have.** 65 measured 4 against 8 against 16 training worlds and
  found no difference at 400 episodes, and the natural reading is that world variety is exhausted. The other
  reading is that the *number* of worlds was never the lever and the *order* is: prioritized level replay scores
  each world by the learning still available in it and samples the next episode from that score, and the reported
  effect is an emergent curriculum of rising difficulty and a real gain in both sample-efficiency and
  generalization to unseen levels -- which is precisely the held-out battery's question. The score they use is the
  TD error, and the mind already keeps it: `RLS.update` computes the residual `e` every row and decays it into
  `m.err[r]`, so a per-world learning-potential score is a matter of bucketing a number that already exists by the
  world it came from. Cost is a table of one float a world.

  Two rows in the tables above look like this one and are not. 65 varied *quantity* and this varies *order*, so it
  is untouched by that result. And 38's "replay of best matches" lost, but that replayed *transitions*; the whole
  claim of the level-replay work is that the level, not the transition, is the right granularity, because
  revisiting a level generates genuinely new episodes rather than re-fitting old rows -- and re-fitting old rows is
  exactly what 37's forgetting result says this covariance will not tolerate. It also upgrades something already
  kept rather than adding a tier: the curriculum (38) is a fixed hand-made weak-then-strong ordering, and this is
  the same idea with the ordering learned. Most direct test: score by decayed `err` a world, sample the next
  episode proportionally, and read the held-out battery. If it does nothing, 65's pessimistic reading is right and
  world selection closes as a line.

- **Distil for adaptability, not for correctness.** 63 is the sharpest negative in the tables: distilled and
  method-only files are no better than a newborn on held-out worlds, even though the distilled file is a large win
  on worlds it knows (39-40, spawns 764 against 572 blank). Distillation takes a trained creature's *final* top
  weights, which optimises for being right, and 63 says being right about world 2 carries nothing to world 24. But
  there is a second objective, and first-order meta-learning is built entirely on the difference: repeatedly move
  an initialisation toward the weights a *short* run on a sampled task reaches, iterated across tasks. The Taylor
  analysis of that update shows it maximises the inner product between the gradients of different tasks -- it
  selects for weights whose *corrections* transfer rather than whose *values* do, and it lands near all the tasks'
  solutions at once rather than at any one of them. That is exactly the property 63 measured as missing, and it is
  the value-side counterpart of 73, where transfer worked as soon as it was carried at tag level instead of as
  numbers.

  Mechanically this is cheap and it fits the existing file: keep a running init and after each training world do
  `w0 <- w0 + eps * (w_world - w0)`, then ship `w0` as the newborn file's priors at the confidence the file already
  uses. It is a weighted average maintained across runs, not new machinery in the creature, and nothing about the
  spawn path changes. Distinguish it from three things that lost: 38's memory sharing and inheritance, 61's spawn
  from the source's covariance, and 62's covariance pooling all mixed state *among peers in a world*, and two of
  the three mixed the covariance. This mixes weights only, never the covariance -- the newborn still starts with a
  fresh `P = I/ridge` and earns its confidence -- and it mixes *across worlds*, which is the axis none of those
  touched. The kept distilled file already proves weight transfer works when the target world is known, so weight
  transfer as such is not what 63 killed; the objective was. Test is a direct swap: the same held-out battery, the
  same file format, `w0` from the meta-average instead of from the best final creature.
